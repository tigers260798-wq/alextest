# Dentures or Dental Implants After 60: What Each Option Costs in 2026 and What Medicare Advantage May Cover

Replacing missing teeth is one of the largest health-related purchases many older adults make, and the price range is enormous: from about $1,500 for a basic denture to tens of thousands of dollars for a full arch of implant-supported teeth. Coverage is just as uneven. This guide compares the main options, gives typical 2026 price ranges and explains what Medicare and Medicare Advantage plans usually do and do not pay for.

## Dentures and implants: how the options differ

There are three broad ways to replace teeth:
- removable dentures, full or partial, which rest on the gums and come out for cleaning;
- dental implants, small titanium posts placed in the jawbone that act as artificial roots for a crown or a bridge;
- implant-supported dentures, a full set of teeth that clips or screws onto several implants.

Dentures cost less and need no surgery, but they can move, may need relining as the gums change and often feel less secure when chewing. Implants feel closer to natural teeth and help preserve the jawbone, but they involve surgery, several months of healing and a higher price. Implant-supported dentures sit in between: more stable than a regular denture, less costly than giving every missing tooth its own implant.

## Dental implants cost in 2026

Prices depend on the region, the dentist's experience, the materials and how much preparation the mouth needs. As a broad guide, typical ranges are:
- a conventional full or partial denture: about $1,500 to $3,000 per plate, with basic options lower and premium sets higher;
- a standard implant: roughly $1,600 to $2,200 for the implant itself, with the abutment and crown often priced separately;
- mini implants: often $500 to $1,500 each, mainly used to steady a lower denture;
- a full arch of implant-supported teeth: commonly $10,000 to $30,000 per arch.

Extra steps can add to the bill. Extractions often cost roughly $75 to $650 per tooth depending on complexity, and a bone graft, needed when the jaw has thinned, can add anything from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars per site. A written treatment plan that lists every stage is the only reliable way to compare quotes.

## Full mouth dental implants: what the price includes

Full mouth dental implants usually mean four to six implants per jaw holding a fixed bridge of teeth. A complete quote should include:
- the consultation and 3D scans;
- extractions and any bone grafting;
- the implants and abutments;
- a temporary set of teeth worn during healing;
- the final bridge and its material.

When quotes differ by many thousands of dollars, the difference is often in what is left out: the temporary teeth, grafting, sedation or a cheaper material for the final bridge. Asking for the total for both jaws, and for what happens if an implant does not heal as planned, makes comparisons fairer.

## What Medicare covers, and what it does not

Original Medicare, Parts A and B, generally does not pay for routine dental care, including cleanings, fillings, dentures and implants. It may pay for dental services in a few narrow cases linked to other covered medical treatment, such as dental work needed before certain heart or cancer procedures. Dental care is one of the Medicare services for seniors that causes the most confusion.

The picture for dental implants for seniors on Medicare changes with Medicare Advantage. Many Medicare Advantage plans include dental benefits, but they usually come with:
- an annual dental maximum, often somewhere between $1,000 and $3,000;
- lower coverage percentages for major work such as dentures and implants;
- waiting periods or limits on how often a denture can be replaced;
- a network of participating dentists.

In practice, a plan benefit is more likely to pay for part of a denture or a share of one implant than for a full arch. Stand-alone dental insurance works in a similar way, with annual maximums and waiting periods for major work.

## Other ways to lower the cost

Searches for affordable dental implants near me usually lead to a handful of real options rather than one bargain:
- dental schools and university clinics, where supervised students and residents often charge less, with longer appointments;
- community health centers with sliding-scale fees for some dental services;
- payment plans offered by clinics, which spread the cost over months, though the terms, including any interest, need a careful read;
- dental discount plans, which are not insurance but give reduced fees at participating dentists;
- adult dental benefits under Medicaid in some states, and dental care through veterans' benefits for those who qualify.

A sensible order is an examination and X-rays first, then a written plan for at least two options, for example a denture and an implant-supported denture, and then a comparison of total cost, number of visits, healing time and warranty. A second opinion on a large treatment plan is common and rarely costs much. The best choice is the one that fits the mouth, the budget and the person's willingness to go through surgery and healing.
