# VA Claim Assistance: A Plain Guide to Pension, Disability Pay and Aid and Attendance

Many people who served in the military decades ago never applied for the benefits linked to that service, or applied once, were turned down and never tried again. Families often learn about these programs only when a parent starts to need care. This guide explains in plain language the main VA benefits that matter to older veterans and surviving spouses, and how VA claim assistance works when a family wants help with the paperwork. It is an independent overview and is not published by or connected with any government agency.

## Three benefits that are easy to confuse

Older veterans and their families usually come across three programs:
- Disability compensation, a monthly tax-free payment for a disability linked to military service.
- The Veterans Pension, a needs-based monthly payment for wartime-era veterans with limited income and net worth.
- Aid and Attendance and Housebound allowances, extra monthly amounts added to a pension, or to disability compensation, when someone needs help with daily life.

Each has its own rules, and a person may qualify for one without qualifying for another.

## Disability compensation: service connection and ratings

Disability compensation is paid when an illness or injury was caused or made worse by military service. The condition receives a rating from 0% to 100% in steps of 10%, and the monthly payment grows with the rating and with the number of dependents. Payments range from a modest amount at the lowest paid ratings to several thousand dollars a month at a 100% rating, and the rates are adjusted every year. A condition can also be linked to service many years later: hearing loss, some cancers and conditions tied to exposures during service are common examples of claims filed long after discharge.

## VA pension eligibility: who may qualify

VA pension eligibility rests on several conditions at once:
- service during a recognized wartime period, with a minimum length of active duty in most cases;
- a discharge other than dishonorable;
- age 65 or older, or a permanent and total disability, or living in a nursing home;
- yearly family income and net worth below limits that are updated every year.

Medical expenses that are not reimbursed, such as the cost of in-home care or assisted living, can be subtracted from income. This is why some families are surprised to find that a parent qualifies once care costs are counted. Surviving spouses of wartime veterans may qualify for a separate survivors pension under similar rules. Assets given away or moved shortly before applying can be reviewed and may delay eligibility, so it is wise to get advice before moving money.

## Aid and attendance VA benefits

Aid and attendance VA benefits are added to the pension when a veteran or surviving spouse needs another person's help with everyday activities such as bathing, dressing or eating, has very limited eyesight, or lives in a nursing home because of a disability. A related allowance, Housebound, applies when a disability keeps someone largely confined to the home. These amounts can help pay for a caregiver at home, an assisted living community or a nursing home. A doctor's statement describing the daily help needed is usually a key part of the claim.

## How veterans claims assistance works

The paperwork can be long: service records, medical evidence, financial statements and forms that must match each other. Veterans claims assistance comes from three kinds of accredited helpers:
- Veterans service officers, who work for veterans service organizations or for county and state offices. Their help is free.
- Accredited claims agents, who have passed an exam and are allowed to represent claimants.
- A VA accredited attorney, a lawyer who has been accredited to represent veterans and their families.

Accreditation matters, because only accredited people may prepare, present and pursue a claim on someone else's behalf. As a rule, no one may charge a fee for help with an initial claim; accredited agents and attorneys usually charge only when a decision is appealed or reviewed, and those fees are limited and must be agreed in writing.

## Warning signs of unaccredited help

Some companies offer to "maximize" benefits for an upfront fee or a share of future payments. Warning signs include requests for payment before a claim is filed, promises of a specific rating, pressure to sign quickly, or advice to move savings into financial products sold by the same adviser. Checking that a helper is accredited takes only a few minutes and can spare a family costly mistakes.

## A simple checklist before starting a claim

- Find the discharge papers and any service records.
- Gather medical records that describe current conditions and daily needs.
- List household income, savings and recurring medical or care costs.
- Decide who will help: a service officer, an accredited agent or a VA accredited attorney.
- Keep copies of everything that is sent, with dates.

Benefits are paid only after a claim is approved, and the effective date often depends on when the claim, or a notice of intent to file, was submitted. For families of someone who served, the most useful step is usually the first one: learning which of the three programs could apply and asking an accredited helper to review the case.
