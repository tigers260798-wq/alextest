# Debt Consolidation in Retirement: How Canadians Turn Several Payments Into One, and the 3 Numbers That Show If It Pays

Credit card balances, a line of credit, a car loan and a store card can each look manageable on their own. Together, with different due dates and interest rates, they can take a large bite out of a fixed retirement income. Debt consolidation is one way Canadians simplify this: several debts are replaced by one arrangement with one payment, ideally at a lower overall cost. This guide explains the main options, from a loan to pay off credit cards to formal debt relief, and how to judge whether a particular offer actually saves money.

## What debt consolidation actually means

Consolidation does not make debt disappear. It changes who is owed, the interest rate and the repayment schedule. Done well, it lowers the interest paid and gives a clear end date. Done badly, it stretches the debt over more years, adds fees and leaves the old credit cards free to fill up again.

There are four common routes in Canada:
- a consolidation loan from a bank, credit union or other lender;
- a balance transfer to a card or line of credit with a lower rate;
- a debt management plan through a non-profit credit counselling agency;
- a consumer proposal, a formal legal settlement for larger debts.

## A loan to pay off credit cards: when the numbers work

Standard credit cards in Canada often charge around 20% a year, and many store cards charge closer to 30%. A personal loan at a clearly lower rate, used to clear those balances, can cut the interest bill and replace several minimum payments with one fixed payment and a fixed end date.

A credit card debt consolidation loan makes the most sense when:
- the new rate is well below the average rate on the debts being cleared;
- the term is not much longer than it would take to clear the cards anyway;
- fees, including any charges for paying off existing loans early, are part of the comparison;
- the cleared cards are closed or kept with a low limit, so the balances do not come back.

The simplest test is to compare the total amount repaid, not the monthly payment. A lower monthly figure spread over seven years can end up costing more than the current debts.

Adverts for fast funding personal loans focus on speed. For consolidation, speed matters far less than the rate, the total cost and the lender's reputation. Canada also caps the interest a lender can legally charge at 35% a year, and offers close to that ceiling rarely make consolidation worthwhile.

## Other routes: balance transfers, home equity and credit counselling

A balance transfer card offers a low or zero rate for a promotional period, usually six to twelve months, often with a transfer fee of a few percent. It works for smaller balances that can realistically be cleared before the promotion ends; after that, the rate jumps.

Homeowners sometimes use a home equity line of credit, which usually carries a lower rate than unsecured borrowing. The trade-off is that the home becomes security for the debt. Reverse mortgages, available from age 55, need no regular payments, but the interest builds up and reduces the equity left later, so independent advice is essential.

Credit consolidation does not always mean a new loan. Non-profit credit counselling agencies offer free or low-cost consultations and can set up a debt management plan: the agency agrees reduced or frozen interest with creditors, and one monthly payment goes to the agency, usually over up to five years. No new credit is needed, which suits people whose credit score would not bring a good loan rate.

## Formal debt relief Canada offers: consumer proposals

When debts are too large to repay in full, the main formal debt relief Canada offers is the consumer proposal. It is arranged through a licensed insolvency trustee, who negotiates with creditors to repay part of what is owed, often a substantial reduction, over up to five years, with interest frozen once it is filed. It is a legal process with real consequences: it stays on the credit file for several years, and it suits larger debt loads rather than a couple of credit cards.

## What lenders look at when the income is a pension

Retirement income is income. Lenders generally accept government pensions and benefits, workplace pensions and regular investment withdrawals, and they look at:
- total monthly income and how steady it is;
- the credit score and payment history;
- how much of the income already goes on debt payments;
- the length of the loan, which some lenders weigh against the borrower's age.

Age on its own is rarely the deciding factor, and it is worth comparing a bank or credit union with a long existing relationship against online lenders.

## The 3 numbers that show if it pays

A few warning signs apply to any offer:
- fees charged upfront, before any loan is paid out;
- pressure to sign the same day;
- promises that every debt can be cleared whatever the circumstances;
- short-term, high-cost loans that must be repaid within weeks, which add cost and solve nothing.

Before signing, three numbers settle most decisions: the annual interest rate, the total amount repaid and the date the debt will be gone. If all three beat the current situation, consolidation is doing its job.
