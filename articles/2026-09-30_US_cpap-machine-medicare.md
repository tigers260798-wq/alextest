# CPAP Machines in 2026: What One Costs and How Medicare's 13-Month Rental Works

A CPAP machine is one of the most common pieces of home medical equipment in the United States, and one of the most misunderstood when it comes to cost. The machine, the mask, the supplies and the insurance rules all work differently from what most people expect. This guide explains how CPAP therapy is set up, what a machine and its supplies typically cost in 2026, and how the Medicare rental works step by step.

## How a CPAP machine works

CPAP stands for continuous positive airway pressure. A small bedside unit draws in room air, filters it and sends it at a gentle, steady pressure through a hose to a mask. The pressure helps keep the upper airway open during sleep, which is why CPAP is the standard first option for obstructive sleep apnea, the most common form of the condition.

Modern machines are compact and fairly quiet. Many include a heated humidifier to reduce dryness, a ramp setting that starts at low pressure and builds up slowly, and a data card or wireless connection that records nightly use. Auto-adjusting models change the pressure through the night instead of holding one fixed setting.

## The first step: a sleep study

CPAP is prescription equipment, so it starts with a diagnosis. There are two main routes:
- an overnight study in a sleep lab, where breathing, oxygen levels, heart rate and sleep stages are monitored;
- a sleep apnea test at home, a simpler kit worn for one or more nights that records breathing, airflow and oxygen levels.

A home test is usually cheaper and more comfortable, and it is now a common first step for adults with a typical picture of obstructive sleep apnea. It does not suit everyone: some heart, lung or neurological conditions call for a full lab study. Medicare may cover either type of test when a doctor orders it and the usual Part B rules are met.

## CPAP machine price in 2026

Bought without insurance, the CPAP machine price commonly falls somewhere between about $500 and $1,650, depending on the model and features. Basic fixed-pressure machines sit at the lower end; auto-adjusting units with a built-in humidifier and wireless reporting sit at the upper end.

The machine is only part of the cost. Masks, cushions, tubing and filters wear out and are meant to be replaced on a schedule, so over several years the running cost can come close to the price of the machine itself.

## Is a CPAP machine covered by Medicare? The 13-month path

Durable medical equipment is one of the Medicare services for seniors that people most often misunderstand, and CPAP is a good example. Under Original Medicare, a CPAP machine covered by Medicare falls under Part B, and it is usually rented rather than bought outright. The typical sequence looks like this:
- a visit with a doctor and a sleep test that confirms obstructive sleep apnea;
- a prescription and a supplier enrolled in Medicare;
- a 12-week trial period, during which Medicare generally pays its share of the rental;
- a follow-up visit that documents that the therapy is helping and that the machine is used regularly, often defined as at least four hours a night on about 70% of nights over a 30-day stretch;
- continued rental payments for up to 13 continuous months, after which the machine generally belongs to the patient.

Medicare typically pays 80% of the approved amount after the yearly Part B deductible, and the remaining 20% is the patient's share unless a supplemental plan picks it up. A replacement machine is generally possible after about five years of use. Medicare Advantage plans have to offer at least the same benefits as Original Medicare, but they may use their own supplier networks, prior authorization and copays, so the details depend on the plan.

## CPAP supplies with insurance

Masks and accessories wear out, and most plans allow regular replacements. With CPAP supplies with insurance, the replacement schedule matters as much as the price. Typical Medicare allowances look roughly like this:
- nasal cushions or pillows: up to twice a month, full face mask cushions once a month;
- a mask frame, nasal or full face: about once every three months;
- tubing: about once every three months;
- disposable filters: up to twice a month;
- headgear, chinstrap and the humidifier water chamber: about once every six months.

Private insurers often follow a similar pattern but set their own limits and may require the same supplier for the whole period. Suppliers can usually confirm what a plan allows before shipping.

## Sleep apnea treatment without CPAP

Not everyone gets on with a mask, and questions about sleep apnea treatment without CPAP are common. Depending on the type and severity of the condition, a doctor may discuss:
- an oral appliance, fitted by a dentist, that holds the lower jaw slightly forward during sleep;
- positional therapy for people whose breathing pauses happen mostly when sleeping on their back;
- weight management, which can reduce severity for some people;
- surgery or an implanted device that stimulates the nerve controlling the tongue, usually considered after CPAP has been tried.

Each option has its own criteria, costs and insurance rules, and results differ from person to person, which is why the choice is made with a sleep specialist.

A practical order is a sleep test first, then the prescription, then a comparison of two or three suppliers on price, mask choice, delivery of replacement supplies and help with fitting. The first weeks with a mask usually involve some adjustment, and a good supplier will swap a mask that does not fit. Watching the usage data during the trial period also matters, because under Medicare the rental continues only once regular use has been documented.
