# Immediate Annuity Explained: How an Annuity Retirement Plan Turns Savings Into Monthly Income

One of the hardest questions in retirement is not how much has been saved, but how to turn those savings into income that lasts. Social Security and, for some, a pension cover part of the monthly bills. An annuity retirement plan is one of the few tools designed to cover more of the gap with a regular payment, either for a set number of years or for life. This guide explains how an immediate annuity works, how it differs from other annuities, what it costs and what to check before signing.

## What an annuity is

An annuity is a contract with a life insurance company. The buyer pays a lump sum or a series of payments, and the insurer agrees to pay money back on a schedule. The payments can start right away or years later, and they can last for a fixed period or for as long as the buyer lives. The key idea is that the insurer takes on part of the longevity risk, the risk of living longer than the savings would last.

## Immediate annuity or deferred annuity

- An immediate annuity, also called an income annuity, is bought with one lump sum, and payments begin soon after purchase, usually within a year. Nothing is invested along the way: the buyer simply exchanges a sum of money for a stream of income.
- A deferred annuity has two phases. First comes the accumulation phase, when the money grows for several years or decades without tax on the gains until they are withdrawn. Then comes the payout phase, which the owner can start later by converting the balance into income or by taking withdrawals.

## Fixed, indexed or variable annuity: where the growth comes from

Deferred annuities differ mainly in how the money grows:
- A fixed annuity pays a set interest rate for a stated number of years, similar in spirit to a certificate of deposit issued by an insurer.
- An indexed annuity links the interest it credits to the performance of a stock market index. Gains are usually limited by a cap or a participation rate, and the contract usually sets a minimum so that index losses are not passed on.
- A variable annuity invests in subaccounts that work much like mutual funds. The value rises and falls with the markets, so the growth potential is higher, and so is the risk. Variable annuities also tend to carry the highest fees.

## Payout options

When income begins, the owner chooses how it will be paid:
- life only, the highest monthly payment, which stops at death;
- life with a period certain, which keeps paying a beneficiary if death comes within a chosen number of years, for example 10 or 20;
- joint and survivor, which keeps paying for as long as either spouse is alive, at a lower monthly amount;
- cash refund, which returns to the heirs any part of the purchase price not yet paid out.

The monthly amount depends on the sum invested, the age when payments start, the payout option chosen and interest rates at the time of purchase. In general, the older the buyer and the higher the rates, the larger the monthly payment.

## Costs and trade-offs

Annuities solve one problem and create others, so the trade-offs deserve an honest look:
- Surrender charges. Most deferred annuities charge a fee for withdrawing more than a set amount during the first several years. An immediate annuity usually cannot be cashed in at all once payments start.
- Fees. Variable annuities may charge mortality and expense fees, administrative fees, fund expenses and extra fees for optional riders, which together can take a noticeable share of returns each year.
- Inflation. A level monthly payment buys less every year. Some contracts offer payments that rise each year, but they start lower.
- Taxes. Earnings are taxed as ordinary income when they come out, and withdrawals before age 59½ usually face an extra tax penalty.
- Insurer strength. Every payment depends on the insurer's ability to pay for many years.

## How to compare before you buy an annuity

Anyone planning to buy an annuity is usually advised to compare offers from several insurers, because the income for the same deposit can differ noticeably from one company to another. Lists of the best rated annuities usually rest on two things: the insurer's financial strength rating from independent rating agencies, and the payout or interest rate offered. Both matter, and a slightly higher payment from a weaker company is not always the better deal.

Because annuities are issued by life insurers, the process feels familiar to anyone who has asked an agent to get a life insurance quote: the agent collects the buyer's age, the amount to invest and the preferred payout option, and returns offers from several companies. It helps to ask how the agent is paid, since commissions on some annuities are high, and to request the contract and a full summary of fees in writing. Many states also give buyers a free-look period after purchase, during which the contract can be canceled for a refund.

## Who an annuity tends to suit

An immediate annuity tends to suit retirees who want part of their savings to work like a paycheck, who worry about running out of money in a long retirement and who can keep the rest of their savings available for emergencies. It is usually a poor fit for money that may be needed soon, and many planners suggest converting only part of the savings rather than all of it. Because the decision is hard to undo, many people talk it through with a licensed financial professional before signing.
