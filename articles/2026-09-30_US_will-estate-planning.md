# Online Will Service or Lawyer? A Plain 2026 Guide to Wills, Trusts and Power of Attorney

Many adults in the United States never get around to writing a will, and even fewer set up a power of attorney. The result is often avoidable trouble: a court decides who inherits, or a family cannot pay a parent's bills during an illness. This guide explains in plain language what a will, a power of attorney and a living trust each do, when an online will service is enough and when a lawyer is worth the cost. It is general information, not legal advice, and the rules differ from state to state.

## What a will does, and what happens without one

A will, or last will and testament, says who receives property after death and names an executor, the person who gathers the assets, pays debts and hands out what is left. Parents of young children can also name a guardian in a will.

When someone dies without a valid will, state law decides who inherits, usually in a fixed order: spouse, children, parents, then more distant relatives. That order may not match what the person wanted. An unmarried partner, a stepchild or a close friend can end up with nothing, and a court appoints someone to manage the estate.

A will does not control everything. Life insurance, retirement accounts and bank accounts with a named beneficiary or a payable-on-death instruction pass directly to the person named on the account, whatever the will says. That is why beneficiary forms matter as much as the will itself.

## Power of attorney: who decides when someone cannot

A power of attorney lets a person, called the principal, appoint an agent to act for them while they are alive. There are two main kinds:
- A financial power of attorney covers money and property: paying bills, managing bank accounts, handling taxes or selling a house.
- A healthcare power of attorney, also called a healthcare proxy, names someone to make medical decisions when the person cannot. It is often paired with a living will, which records wishes about treatment but does not name a decision-maker.

To keep working if the principal later loses capacity, a power of attorney must be durable. Some documents are springing, taking effect only after incapacity, but banks and hospitals may ask for proof before accepting them. Every power of attorney ends at death; from that point the will and the executor take over.

Without these documents, a family may need to ask a court to appoint a guardian or conservator, a process that takes time, costs money and is public.

## Living trusts: when a will is not the only tool

A revocable living trust holds property during the owner's lifetime, with the owner usually acting as trustee. After death, a successor trustee distributes the property privately, without probate, the court process that a will goes through. People often consider a trust when they own real estate in more than one state, want to avoid probate delays, have a blended family or want to leave money to someone in stages.

A trust only works for assets actually moved into it, so a simple pour-over will is usually signed at the same time to catch anything left out. Because trusts involve retitling property and more careful drafting, many people prefer to work with a family trust lawyer for this step, especially when a business, rental property or a relative with special needs is involved.

## Online will service or attorney: which fits?

An online will service asks a series of questions and produces a will built for the rules of a chosen state, usually for a one-time fee or a yearly subscription that allows updates. For a straightforward situation, such as a married couple leaving everything to each other and then to their children, this is often enough. Many platforms also offer financial and healthcare forms, so a power of attorney online can be prepared in the same session.

When comparing platforms in search of the best online will and trust option, it helps to check:
- whether documents are tailored to the rules of the state;
- whether a living trust is included or costs extra;
- whether updates are free after a marriage, a divorce or a new grandchild;
- whether an attorney can review the documents if questions come up.

An attorney is usually worth the cost for larger estates, property in several states, a family business, children from different marriages, or any plan to leave a close relative out. Estate planning attorneys may charge a flat fee for a package of documents or bill by the hour.

## Signing it right: witnesses, notaries and state rules

A will signed incorrectly can fail. Most states require the person making it to sign in front of two adult witnesses who do not inherit under the will. Some states also accept a will written entirely by hand without witnesses, while many others do not. Adding a notarized self-proving affidavit, where the state allows it, can make probate faster later. Powers of attorney often have to be notarized, and some banks prefer their own forms, so it makes sense to ask them early.

## Keeping documents up to date

Estate documents are not a one-time task. A review every few years, and after any major event such as a marriage, a divorce, a death in the family or a move to another state, keeps them accurate. Originals should be stored safely, and the executor and agents should know where to find them. A short letter explaining the plan to the family can prevent confusion and disputes at a difficult time.

A basic will, a durable financial power of attorney and a healthcare proxy cover the most common risks for most families. Whether they are prepared online or with a lawyer, the most important step is to sign them while there is still time to choose.
