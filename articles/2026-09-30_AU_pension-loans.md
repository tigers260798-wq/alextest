# Pension Loans in Australia for 2026: How the Home Equity Access Scheme Works and How the Other Options Compare

Pension loans Australia-wide usually mean one of several quite different products. One is a government scheme secured against a home. Another is an early payment of part of the pension itself. Others are ordinary loans from banks, credit unions and community organisations. They differ in cost, in what is used as security and in how they affect what is left for the family later. This guide explains how each option works and what to check before signing anything.

## The Home Equity Access Scheme, formerly the Pension Loans Scheme

The Home Equity Access Scheme is a voluntary loan offered by the federal government and secured against real estate in Australia. Until July 2022 it was called the Pension Loans Scheme, which is why many people still search for the pension loans scheme under its old name.

- Who can use it: people who have reached Age Pension age, or whose partner has, and who either receive a qualifying pension or would qualify except for the income or assets test. That includes many self-funded retirees.
- How it is paid: as regular fortnightly payments. The pension and the loan together can reach up to one and a half times the maximum pension rate. Lump-sum advances are also possible, up to half of the maximum annual pension rate in any 26-week period.
- What it costs: interest at a rate set by the government, 3.95% a year at the time of writing, compounding fortnightly. The balance grows over time, interest included.
- Repayment: the loan can be repaid in part or in full at any time, and is often repaid when the property is sold or from the estate.
- Protection: negative equity protection means the debt cannot grow beyond the value of the property, provided the loan conditions are met.

The maximum loan depends on the age of the borrower, or the younger partner, and on the equity in the property. Borrowers can also set aside part of the property's value so that it is never used as security.

## Centrelink advance payment: part of the pension, paid early

A Centrelink advance payment is not a loan with interest. It lets pension recipients receive part of their future pension early, as a lump sum, and repay it through small deductions from later payments, generally over about six months. There are minimum and maximum amounts, and another advance is usually not available until the previous one is repaid.

Of all the Centrelink loans for pensioners people ask about, only the Home Equity Access Scheme charges interest. The advance payment suits a one-off expense, such as a new appliance or a car repair, but it reduces income for the following months, which is worth planning for.

## Loans for pensioners on Centrelink: bank, credit union and community options

Outside the government scheme, loans for pensioners on Centrelink come from three main places:

- Banks and credit unions. Lenders can count pension income, but they must check that repayments are affordable over the whole term. Personal loans usually run for one to seven years, and the comparison rate, which includes most fees, is the figure to compare.
- Community no-interest loan programs. Community organisations offer loans with no interest and no fees for essentials such as fridges, washing machines, car repairs or dental bills, for people on lower incomes, including many pension and concession card holders. Repayments are spread over roughly a year to eighteen months.
- Credit cards and buy now, pay later accounts. They are convenient for small purchases but can become expensive if balances are carried forward.

Very short loans with high fees are the costliest way to borrow and tend to make a tight budget tighter; a longer, lower-cost option is usually easier to manage.

## Private reverse mortgages and home reversion

Some private lenders offer reverse mortgages, usually from age 60. Like the government scheme, they are secured against the home and repaid when it is sold, and they carry negative equity protection by law. The main differences: the borrower can often take a larger lump sum or a line of credit, interest rates are usually noticeably higher, and there may be establishment and ongoing fees.

Home reversion works differently. The homeowner sells a share of the future sale price of the property to a provider at a discount, in exchange for a lump sum now. There is no interest, but the share given up is usually worth considerably more than the cash received.

## How the options compare

- Cost: the government scheme charges a rate set by the government; private reverse mortgages usually cost more; advance payments and community no-interest loans charge no interest; bank loans depend on the lender and the borrower's history.
- Security: the government scheme, reverse mortgages and home reversion are tied to the home; the other options are not.
- Purpose: advance payments and community loans are designed for one-off expenses; home-secured options suit regular extra income over years.
- Effect on the estate: every home-secured option reduces the equity left for the family, and compounding interest makes the difference larger over time.

## Questions to ask before signing

- What is the total cost over the whole term, not only the interest rate?
- How much could the balance grow in five, ten and fifteen years?
- Can the loan be repaid early without a penalty?
- What happens if the borrower moves into aged care or sells the home?
- Has the family been told, and has independent legal or financial advice been taken?

Free, independent financial counselling services can help compare the options before any decision. A pension loan can smooth income in retirement, but it is a long commitment, and it is worth understanding fully before signing.
