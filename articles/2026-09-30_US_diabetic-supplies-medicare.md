# Diabetic Supplies in 2026: What Medicare Usually Pays For, From Test Strips to a CGM Device

Blood sugar checks are a daily routine for millions of older Americans, and the supplies behind them add up: meters, test strips, lancets, sensors and, for some people, special footwear. The coverage rules are detailed, and small differences, such as whether someone uses insulin, change how many supplies a plan will pay for. This guide walks through the most common diabetic supplies, what Medicare usually covers and under which conditions, and where out-of-pocket costs tend to appear.

## The main diabetic supplies

Most people who check their blood sugar use one of two systems:
- a blood glucose meter, which reads a small drop of blood placed on a test strip, with a lancet device to draw the drop;
- a continuous glucose monitor, a small sensor worn on the skin that measures glucose around the clock and sends readings to a reader or a smartphone.

How often someone tests, and which system suits them, is a decision made with their doctor. It usually depends on the type of diabetes, the treatment and whether insulin is part of it.

## Diabetic test strips: how many Medicare usually allows

Under Part B, Original Medicare treats meters, diabetic test strips, lancets and control solution as durable medical equipment supplies. Coverage generally requires a doctor's prescription and a supplier or pharmacy enrolled in Medicare. The standard quantities depend on insulin use:
- for people who use insulin, up to 300 test strips and 300 lancets every three months;
- for people who do not use insulin, up to 100 test strips and 100 lancets every three months.

A doctor can request more when there is a documented medical need, for example more frequent testing during a change of treatment. Testing records may be asked for in that case.

## Continuous glucose monitor: when a CGM device may be covered

The continuous glucose monitor has become one of the most searched diabetes products, and Medicare rules for it have widened in recent years. A CGM device and its sensors may be covered under Part B when, broadly:
- a doctor has diagnosed diabetes and prescribes the device;
- the person uses insulin, or has a documented history of problematic low blood sugar;
- there has been a visit with the prescribing clinician in the six months before the order, with regular follow-up visits after that.

Sensors are replaced every one to two weeks depending on the model, so the ongoing supply matters more than the reader. Some systems pair with a phone instead of a separate reader, which can lower the upfront cost. Medicare Advantage plans generally cover the same categories but may prefer certain brands, suppliers or pharmacy channels.

## What people usually pay

For Part B supplies, Medicare typically pays 80% of the approved amount after the yearly Part B deductible, and the remaining 20% is the patient's share unless a supplemental policy picks it up. A supplier that accepts assignment usually keeps that share at the standard rate. Insulin itself is handled differently: under Medicare, the monthly cost of each covered insulin is capped at $35.

Supplies are among the Medicare services for seniors that most often cause billing surprises. The usual reasons are an out-of-network supplier, an expired prescription or an order that goes beyond the standard quantity.

## Free blood glucose meter offers: where the catch is

Searches for a free blood glucose meter lead to plenty of offers, and many are genuine: meter makers and pharmacies often give away or heavily discount the meter itself. The reason is simple. The meter is a one-time purchase, while each meter works only with its own brand of test strips, and the strips are the ongoing cost. Before accepting a free meter, it makes sense to check three things:
- the price of its test strips per box;
- whether the plan covers that brand of strips;
- whether the usual pharmacy or supplier keeps them in stock.

## Shoes, socks and other extras

Not every product marketed for diabetes falls under standard coverage. Ordinary diabetic or compression socks are generally not paid for by Original Medicare, although some Medicare Advantage plans include an allowance for them. Therapeutic shoes are different: for people with diabetes-related foot problems, Part B may cover one pair of extra-depth or custom-molded shoes with inserts per calendar year, when the doctor managing the diabetes certifies the need and a podiatrist or another qualified specialist prescribes them.

A short checklist for diabetic supplies covered by Medicare:
- a current prescription that states the testing frequency;
- a Medicare-enrolled supplier or pharmacy;
- quantities within the standard limits, or documentation for more;
- a check of the plan's preferred brands before switching meters or sensors.

The simplest start is a conversation with the doctor about testing frequency and devices, then a comparison of two or three suppliers on brands, delivery schedules and the share left to pay. Many suppliers check coverage before the first shipment. A review of the plan each year during open enrollment also helps, because preferred brands and supplier networks can change.
