# The 5-Minute Memory Check Doctors Use After 60 – and What Memory Care Costs in 2026

Forgetting a name or where the car keys ended up is part of getting older. Doctors separate that everyday forgetfulness from early warning signs with a short check that takes about five minutes: drawing a clock and remembering three words. Below is how the check works, how it is scored, which changes doctors look for, and what dementia memory care costs in 2026 – from assisted living with memory care to nursing homes – including what Medicare pays and what it does not.

## Clock drawing and 3 words: how the 5-minute memory check works

In clinics the check is known as the Mini-Cog. It has three steps:

- Three words: the doctor names three unrelated everyday words, for example apple, river and table, and asks for them to be repeated right away.
- The clock: on a blank circle, the person writes in all the numbers of a clock face and then sets the hands to a given time, usually ten past eleven.
- Recall: after the clock is finished, the doctor asks for the three words again, without hints.

The clock step is the clever part. It tests planning, attention and spatial sense at once, and it keeps the three words from being silently rehearsed.

## How the check is scored – and what it can and cannot show

The score runs from 0 to 5:

- 1 point for each word recalled, up to 3 points
- 2 points for a clock with all numbers in the right places and the hands showing the right time, 0 points if not

In the common scoring, a total of 3 to 5 makes significant memory problems less likely, while 0 to 2 usually leads to a longer evaluation. A low score is not a diagnosis. Poor sleep, low mood, hearing problems, low vitamin B12, thyroid problems and side effects of medicines can all pull results down, and several of these are treatable. A longer evaluation typically includes more detailed memory tests, blood work and sometimes a brain scan.

## Normal forgetfulness or something more: what doctors look for

Doctors compare what happens occasionally with what is new and keeps repeating:

- Normal: forgetting a name and remembering it later. Worth a check: asking the same question again within minutes.
- Normal: misplacing glasses now and then. Worth a check: putting things in unusual places, such as keys in the fridge.
- Normal: needing a moment to find a word. Worth a check: regularly losing the thread of a conversation.
- Normal: missing a monthly payment once. Worth a check: new trouble with bills, recipes or other familiar tasks.
- Normal: feeling lost in an unfamiliar city. Worth a check: getting lost on a route used for years.

Changes in mood, withdrawal from hobbies and unusual decisions about money are also on the list. Relatives often notice these signs before the person does.

## Dementia memory care: assisted living, nursing homes and the difference

When living at home is no longer safe, dementia memory care is the step many families look at next. There are three common settings:

- Assisted living with memory care: a separate, secured wing inside an assisted living community, with staff trained in dementia, structured days and help with bathing, dressing and medicines.
- Stand-alone memory care communities: built only for dementia, often with circular hallways, enclosed gardens and more staff per resident.
- Nursing homes with memory care: for residents who also need daily medical or nursing care, with licensed nurses on site around the clock.

Questions worth asking on a visit: how many staff are on duty at night, what dementia training they have, how exits are secured, and what happens when care needs increase.

## Memory care facility cost per month in 2026

Memory care costs more than regular assisted living because of the extra staff and security. In 2026 the national average is about $7,645 a month. The gap between states is wide: from about $5,538 a month in South Dakota, the least expensive state, to about $14,399 in Hawaii, the most expensive.

When comparing the memory care facility cost per month, it pays to check what the price includes:

- a flat rate versus care levels that rise as needs increase
- one-time entrance or community fees
- medication management, incontinence supplies and escorts to appointments
- the rules for rate increases, which usually come once a year

## Medicare services for seniors: what is paid and what is not

Medicare services for seniors cover much of the medical side of memory loss, but not the cost of living in memory care.

- Covered: the yearly wellness visit, which includes a check of memory and thinking; a separate visit for a detailed cognitive assessment and care plan; doctor visits, tests and scans; hospice care.
- Partly covered: up to 100 days in a skilled nursing facility after a hospital stay of at least three days, with the first 20 days fully covered and a daily copay after that.
- Not covered: long-term custodial care – room, meals and daily help in assisted living, memory care or a nursing home.

Families also pay with long-term care insurance, veterans' benefits and, for people with limited income and savings, Medicaid, which in many states helps cover memory care through waiver programs with their own rules and waiting lists. In Canada and the UK, care is organized and funded differently, and what a family pays depends on the province or local council and on income and savings.

The 5-minute check does not replace a full evaluation, but it gives families and doctors a starting point. Early answers leave more time to compare care options and costs calmly.
