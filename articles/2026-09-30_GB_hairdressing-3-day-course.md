# 3 Day Hairdressing Course: What Three Days Cover, What It Costs and What Comes Next

A 3 day hairdressing course is one of the quickest ways to find out whether cutting and styling hair is enjoyable in practice. Among hairdressing courses for adults, short formats like this are popular because they fit into a long weekend or a few days of annual leave. They teach real hands-on skills, but they are not a hairdressing qualification. This guide explains what is usually covered in three days, who such courses suit, what they cost, how online and part-time options compare and which route leads to a recognised qualification.

## What a 3 day hairdressing course usually covers

Most three-day courses follow a similar plan, with a short demonstration by the tutor followed by practice on a training head, a realistic mannequin with human or synthetic hair.

- Day one: tools and hygiene, how to hold scissors and comb, a client consultation, sectioning the hair and a first basic cut such as a one-length bob.
- Day two: layered cuts, blow-drying with a round brush, curling and straightening, and a simple up-do.
- Day three: colour basics, including how shades and tones work, a practical exercise on the training head, finishing and aftercare advice.

Groups are usually small, often four to eight people, so the tutor can correct technique at each station. Many courses include the training head and a starter kit that learners take home. At the end there is normally a certificate of attendance, which confirms the training took place but is not a formal qualification.

## Short hairdressing courses: who they suit

Short hairdressing courses tend to attract a few groups of people:

- beginners who want to test their interest before committing to a year of study;
- parents who would like to cut their children's hair neatly at home;
- adults thinking about a change of direction who want a realistic taste of the work;
- former hairdressers returning after a break who need a refresher on current techniques;
- people interested in barbering, for whom many centres run a separate short barbering course.

They are less suitable for anyone expecting to work on paying clients after three days. Salon work requires much more practice time and, in most cases, a recognised qualification.

## Level 2 hairdressing course: the step to a recognised qualification

A level 2 hairdressing course is where formal training usually starts. It covers cutting, colouring, styling and finishing, consultation, health and safety and working in a salon environment. Assessment includes practical work on real clients as well as written or online tests.

Duration depends on the format. Full-time study at a college often takes around one academic year, part-time study one to two years, and some private academies run intensive programmes of several months. After Level 2, learners can move on to Level 3, which adds advanced cutting, colour correction and more complex styles. Hours spent on a short course do not normally count towards these qualifications, but they can make the first weeks of a longer course easier.

## Hairdressing course online: what can be learned on screen

A hairdressing course online works well for theory and demonstrations: colour chemistry, face shapes, consultation, product knowledge and step-by-step videos of cuts and styles. Many online courses send a kit with a training head so that learners can practise at home.

The limit is feedback. Without a tutor standing at the station, small mistakes in angle, tension or sectioning often go unnoticed. For that reason, many people combine an online course with a few practical days in a training centre, and online certificates are usually certificates of completion rather than qualifications.

## Hairdressing courses for adults part time: evenings and weekends

For adults who work or care for family, hairdressing courses for adults part time are the most common route to a qualification. Typical formats are one or two evening sessions a week at a college, weekend blocks at a private academy or one day a week alongside a job. Part-time learners usually need to bring their own models, such as friends or relatives, for practical assessments, so it helps to plan who can sit in the chair over the months of the course.

## What a short course costs and what to check

As a broad guide, a three-day course often costs around £250 to £700, depending on group size and whether a kit and training head are included. A part-time Level 2 course typically costs from about £1,000 to £3,500. Online courses are the cheapest option, often around £50 to £300, or more when a kit and training head are posted out. Whether the kit and practice heads are included or charged extra makes a real difference: a professional kit alone can add £150 to £500. Some adults can get help with fees depending on age, income and previous qualifications.

Before booking, it is worth checking:

- the tutor's salon and teaching experience;
- the number of learners per tutor;
- how much of each day is hands-on practice;
- whether training heads, models and a kit are included;
- which certificate is issued at the end.

Claims that three days make someone ready to work in a salon deserve caution. A short course is a good first step: it shows whether the craft is enjoyable, teaches the basics safely and makes the choice of a longer course easier. Salon placements, work and pay are not part of most short courses and no course can promise them.
