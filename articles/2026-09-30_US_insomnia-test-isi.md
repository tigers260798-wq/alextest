# The 7-Question Insomnia Test Sleep Specialists Use (ISI): What Each Score Means and How Treatment Without Sleeping Pills Works

Lying awake at 3 a.m. now and then is common. Sleep specialists separate an occasional bad night from insomnia that needs attention with a short questionnaire: seven questions, each scored from 0 to 4, for a total between 0 and 28. It is called the Insomnia Severity Index, or ISI. Below: the seven questions, how the score is read, what it cannot show, and how insomnia treatment without sleeping pills works – from cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia to sleep restriction therapy.

## The Insomnia Severity Index: 7 questions about the last two weeks

The ISI looks back over the previous two weeks. Each answer is a number from 0 to 4:

- 1. Difficulty falling asleep: from 0 (none) to 4 (very severe).
- 2. Difficulty staying asleep through the night: 0 to 4.
- 3. Waking up too early and not getting back to sleep: 0 to 4.
- 4. Satisfaction with the current sleep pattern: from 0 (very satisfied) to 4 (very dissatisfied).
- 5. How noticeable the sleep problem is to other people, in terms of quality of life: 0 to 4.
- 6. How worried or distressed the person is about the sleep problem: 0 to 4.
- 7. How much the sleep problem interferes with daily life – energy, mood, concentration, memory, work: 0 to 4.

The first three questions describe the night itself. The last four describe how much the nights affect the day, which is often what brings people to a doctor.

## How the 0–28 score is read – and why it is not a diagnosis

The seven answers are added up:

- 0 to 7: no clinically significant insomnia
- 8 to 14: subthreshold insomnia, a problem worth watching
- 15 to 21: clinical insomnia of moderate severity
- 22 to 28: severe clinical insomnia

The ISI is a self-assessment tool. It measures how severe sleep problems feel, not what causes them. The same score can come from stress, pain, a snoring partner, shift work, too much caffeine, low mood, or a sleep disorder such as sleep apnea or restless legs, and each of these needs different care. That is why doctors use the score as a starting point for a conversation, not as a diagnosis, and why a score in the higher ranges is a reason to book an appointment rather than to self-treat.

## Short-term or chronic: what sleep specialists look at next

Specialists usually ask for a sleep diary kept for one or two weeks: bedtime, how long it took to fall asleep, awakenings, the final wake-up time, naps, caffeine and alcohol. Two questions then matter most:

- How often: insomnia counts as chronic when the problem appears on at least three nights a week.
- How long: the problem has lasted for at least three months, with tiredness, irritability or poor concentration during the day.

Short-term insomnia after a move, a loss or a stressful week is common and often passes on its own. Chronic insomnia tends to keep itself going: worry about sleep, extra time in bed and irregular hours become part of the problem. Many insomnia remedies people try first – a nightcap, a late nap, going to bed earlier, lying in at weekends – can make it last longer, because alcohol breaks up sleep and extra hours in bed weaken the natural drive to sleep.

## Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia: how CBT-I works, week by week

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, or CBT-I, is the approach doctors usually recommend first for chronic insomnia in adults, ahead of sleeping pills. A typical program runs for four to eight weekly sessions, one to one, in a group or online, and combines several parts:

- Sleep restriction therapy: time in bed is limited to the time actually spent asleep, based on the diary – for example 6 hours instead of 8 – but usually not below 5 to 5.5 hours. When most of the time in bed is spent asleep, the window is extended by about 15 minutes a week.
- Stimulus control: the bed is used only for sleep; after about 20 minutes awake, the person gets up, does something calm in dim light and returns when sleepy. The wake-up time stays the same every day, weekends included.
- Cognitive work: unhelpful beliefs such as "without 8 hours, tomorrow is ruined" are examined and replaced with more realistic ones.
- Sleep hygiene and relaxation: caffeine after midday, alcohol, late heavy meals, bright screens and a warm bedroom are reviewed, and breathing and muscle relaxation are practised.

The first weeks of sleep restriction are often the hardest, with more daytime sleepiness, so specialists advise against driving while drowsy and adapt the plan for some people, for example those with epilepsy or bipolar disorder, or older adults at risk of falls. Progress usually builds over several weeks rather than overnight.

## Insomnia treatment options: family doctor, sleep clinic or online CBT-I

- Family doctor or GP: the usual first step. The doctor reviews current medicines, checks for pain, thyroid problems, low mood or signs of sleep apnea, and can refer on.
- Sleep clinic: for suspected sleep apnea, restless legs or insomnia that has not improved; the visit may include an overnight sleep study.
- Psychologist trained in CBT-I: individual sessions in person or by video. In the US, health insurance often covers part of the cost, depending on the plan.
- Online CBT-I programs: structured courses with a sleep diary and weekly steps, sometimes offered through a doctor, an employer or an insurer.

In the UK, a GP can point to talking therapy services and digital CBT-I options available through the NHS in many areas. In Canada and Australia, provincial plans, Medicare rebates or private health insurance may cover part of psychologist sessions, depending on referral rules.

The seven questions take about two minutes. A written score and a sleep diary of one or two weeks give a doctor a clear picture and make the first appointment more useful.
