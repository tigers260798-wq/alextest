# Knee Pain Relief After 60: What Actually Helps, and What a Private Knee Replacement Costs in 2026

Stiff, aching joints are one of the most common reasons older adults cut back on walking, gardening or travel. Knees and hips are affected most often, and the usual cause is osteoarthritis, the gradual wear of the cartilage that cushions the ends of the bones. There is no single fix, but there is a well-established ladder of options, from simple daily habits to surgery. This guide explains each step, what it can and cannot do, and what the private route costs for those who do not want to wait. It is general information, not a diagnosis: a GP or physiotherapist is the right person to assess any individual joint.

## Why knees and hips stiffen with age

Osteoarthritis develops slowly. The cartilage thins, the joint lining can become inflamed and small bony growths may form at the edges of the joint. Typical signs are stiffness after rest, aching after activity and a grinding or crackling feeling when moving. Rheumatoid arthritis is different: it is an autoimmune condition that tends to affect several joints at once, often the small joints of the hands and feet, and it is treated by specialists with its own medicines. Because the two need different treatment, new, persistent or worsening joint pain is worth having checked rather than guessed at.

## Everyday knee pain relief: movement comes first

It sounds backwards, but for osteoarthritis the most useful long-term knee pain relief usually comes from moving more, not less. Stronger thigh and hip muscles take load off the knee, and regular movement keeps the joint supple. The approaches with the best track record are:
- strengthening exercises, ideally planned by a physiotherapist and done a few times a week;
- low-impact activity such as walking, swimming, cycling or water aerobics;
- keeping body weight in a healthy range, since extra weight adds load to the knees with every step;
- supportive, cushioned shoes and, where needed, a walking stick held in the hand opposite the painful knee;
- heat packs for stiffness and cold packs for flare-ups after activity.

A flare-up is not a reason to stop for good. Pacing, splitting a long walk into shorter ones and resting between tasks lets many people stay active on bad days.

## Painkillers, gels and injections: what doctors usually offer

Medicines ease symptoms rather than repair the joint, so they work best alongside exercise. The usual options are:
- anti-inflammatory gels or creams rubbed into the knee, often suggested first because little of the medicine reaches the rest of the body;
- anti-inflammatory tablets for short periods, sometimes with a stomach-protecting medicine, since they can irritate the stomach and do not suit everyone;
- steroid injections into the joint for a bad flare-up, which can ease pain for a few weeks to a couple of months but are not a long-term answer.

A pharmacist can explain which products are safe alongside other regular medicines, which matters for anyone taking blood thinners or blood pressure tablets.

## Joint pain relief supplements: what is known

Shelves are full of joint pain relief supplements, most often glucosamine, chondroitin, turmeric and fish oil. The honest picture is that the evidence is mixed: some people report a benefit, but larger trials have not shown a clear effect for most of these products, and none of them replaces exercise or medical advice. Glucosamine can interact with some blood-thinning medicines, and turmeric can affect others, so a quick word with a pharmacist before starting is sensible. People who decide to try a supplement often give it a fixed trial of around three months and stop if nothing changes.

## Arthritis treatment knee specialists suggest when the basics are not enough

When pain keeps limiting walking or sleep despite exercise and medicines, a GP can refer to physiotherapy or to an orthopaedic clinic. The arthritis treatment knee specialists suggest at that stage may include a structured exercise programme, a brace or insoles, or a discussion about surgery. Keyhole washing-out of an arthritic knee is no longer recommended for osteoarthritis, so the main surgical option for advanced wear is joint replacement, either partial or total.

A knee replacement is a major operation with a long recovery. Most people spend a few days in hospital and need weeks of exercises afterwards, and full recovery can take several months or longer. Surgeons weigh the likely benefit against general health and how much the knee limits daily life.

## Private knee replacement cost vs the NHS waiting list

On the NHS a knee or hip replacement is free, but orthopaedic waiting lists are among the longest, and waits of many months are common. That is why interest in the private knee replacement cost has grown among people who would rather not wait.

A private total knee replacement in the UK typically costs somewhere between £12,000 and £16,000 as an all-in package, with hip replacements in a similar range. When quotes are compared, it is worth checking whether the price covers:
- the first consultation and scans;
- the operation, the implant and the hospital stay;
- physiotherapy after discharge;
- follow-up appointments and treatment of any complications.

Some people use private medical insurance, which may cover the operation if the policy was in place before the knee problem began. Others pay for a single private consultation to get a clear plan and then decide whether to wait or to pay.

## The bottom line

Joint pain in later life is usually managed rather than cured, and the mix that helps many people is steady exercise, a healthy weight and sensible use of pain relief. Surgery is the last step rather than the first, and when it comes, knowing the real cost of each route makes the choice easier.
